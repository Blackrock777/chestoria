## Eight new source references

### Gesù Nuovo

**Hook:** A prince’s palace hiding in plain sight?

**Story (36 words):** In 1470, this was a prince’s palace, not a church. When the Jesuits transformed it in the 1580s, they kept its diamond-shaped stone façade. Look closely: some stones still bear marks made by the original masons.

**Added sources:**
- Campania CRBC — Centro Regionale Beni Culturali: https://www.campaniacrbc.it/portal/generaDettaglio.do?idPagina=20H721&l=it&tipoScheda=LC — Conversion from Palazzo Sanseverino beginning 1584; diamond-point stone façade; modern church
- Bollettino Telematico dell’Arte — academic art-history essay: https://www.bta.it/txt/a0/07/bta00708.html — 1470 inscription naming Novello da San Lucano, 1584 Jesuit acquisition, preservation of exterior stonework

**Fact-check statements:**
- Palazzo Sanseverino was commissioned for Roberto Sanseverino and dates to 1470  (sources: 1, 2, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- The Jesuits began adapting the palace as a church in 1584  (sources: 1, 3, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- The church retained the diamond-point piperno stone façade of the palace  (sources: 1, 2, 3, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- Masons’ marks remain visible on the diamond-point façade  (sources: 2; single_source_supported_editorial_review_pending)
- The church was consecrated on 7 October 1601  (sources: 1; single_source_supported_editorial_review_pending)

**Tourist/rights warning:** Exterior stone façade visible from Piazza del Gesù Nuovo; individual mason marks need on-site angle/visibility check. No photo needed for the story. Linked institutional and academic pages are research references only. Do not copy their written descriptions or images into training material.

**Unresolved:**
- A Jesuit organizational article gives 1597 for acquisition, while the Ministry, regional catalogue and scholarly essay reference 1584 for acquisition or start of conversion. Phrase CheStoria as “conversion began in 1584” and do not give an unqualified acquisition date until a historian reconciles it. The Jesuit page: https://gesuiti.it/gesu-nuovo-la-facciata-di-diamanti-torna-a-brillare/

### Galleria Borbonica

**Hook:** A royal passage that became a wartime shelter?

**Story (34 words):** In 1853, King Ferdinand II ordered this underground passage through Naples. Decades later, the tunnel and nearby cisterns sheltered residents during Second World War air raids. One underground space served two very different eras.

**Added sources:**
- FAI — Fondo per l’Ambiente Italiano, I Luoghi del Cuore: https://fondoambiente.it/luoghi/galleria-borbonica — 1853 decree, architect Errico Alvino, planned royal escape route, Second World War shelter, postwar municipal storage
- Campania CRBC — Centro Regionale Beni Culturali: https://www.campaniacrbc.it/portal/generaDettaglio.do?idPagina=20H17353&tipoScheda=RIFTER — Ferdinand II, 1853 commission, WWII shelter, underground heritage features, published entrances

**Fact-check statements:**
- King Ferdinand II commissioned an underground route in 1853  (sources: 1, 2, 3, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- Architect Errico Alvino was tasked with the tunnel project  (sources: 1, 2, 3, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- The project proposed a military connection and possible escape for the royal family  (sources: 2, 3; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- Underground spaces were converted into air-raid shelters during World War II  (sources: 1, 2, 3, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- The gallery and nearby rooms served as municipal judicial storage after the war  (sources: 1, 2, 3; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- The regional heritage listing identifies multiple visitor entrances rather than one tunnel midpoint  (sources: 4; single_source_supported_editorial_review_pending)

**Tourist/rights warning:** Use map-first preview; visiting requires a guided tour/access arrangement. The tunnel is underground and an exterior photo cannot identify the interior scene. Research-linked FAI and regional pages; no permissions assumed for text, photographs or commercial audioguides.

**Unresolved:**
- Wikidata tunnel coordinates identify the site, NOT a usable entrance. Opening routes and exact entry points vary by operator and tour; verify through the venue at visit time.
- Avoid quoting “5,000–10,000 people sheltered” as a uniquely measured attendance statistic; it appears in operator and Royal District material and is an estimate.

### Galleria Umberto I

**Hook:** A whole zodiac hidden beneath your feet?

**Story (35 words):** Built between 1887 and 1890, Galleria Umberto I replaced unhealthy old streets with a grand shopping arcade. Under its iron-and-glass dome, the floor hides zodiac mosaics and a compass rose. Look down before looking up.

**Added sources:**
- Campania CRBC — Centro Regionale Beni Culturali: https://www.campaniacrbc.it/portal/generaDettaglio.do?idPagina=20H700&l=it&tipoScheda=LC — Iron-and-glass dome, height reported as 56.7 m, zodiac floor mosaics, public arcade
- Discover Campania — architectural and cultural feature: https://discovercampania.it/en/explore-campania/territory/naples/go-see-naples/squares/1182-the-umberto-i-gallery — Urban redevelopment of 1887–1890, four arcade arms, zodiac and compass rose floor, accessible entrances

**Fact-check statements:**
- Galleria Umberto I was built between 1887 and 1890  (sources: 1, 2, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- The gallery formed part of urban renewal replacing unhealthy old streets near Teatro San Carlo  (sources: 1, 2, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- Its central roof structure is made of iron and glass  (sources: 1, 3, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- The floor includes mosaics of zodiac signs  (sources: 1, 3, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- A compass rose is set within the floor mosaics  (sources: 1, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- The regional heritage listing gives the dome height as 56.7 metres  (sources: 3; single_source_supported_editorial_review_pending)

**Tourist/rights warning:** Public arcade, but the zodiac challenge is INSIDE under the dome, not visible on its exterior. Mark optional indoor look-closer without mandatory photo upload. Discover Campania explicitly prohibits reproduction without permission; use as verification reference only. Regional catalogue usage and media licenses must be checked separately.

**Unresolved:**
- Some popular accounts assign different opening dates; retain only the widely documented 1887–1890 construction span until official inauguration archive is checked.
- Discover Campania says mosaics were relocated after World War II; do not imply every mosaic element visible today is the untouched 1890 original.

### Museum of the Treasure of San Gennaro

**Hook:** A necklace assembled across generations?

**Story (36 words):** San Gennaro’s jeweled necklace began in 1679 and gained new gems through later donations. Kings, queens and ordinary devotees contributed gifts over generations. The treasure is both an artwork and a record of the city’s devotion.

**Added sources:**
- Italian Ministry of Culture — Museo del Tesoro di San Gennaro: https://cultura.gov.it/luogo/museo-del-tesoro-di-san-gennaro — Museum opened in December 2003, Deputazione founded 1601, centuries of gifts and artistic collections
- RAI Cultura — I Tesori di San Gennaro: https://www.raicultura.it/arte/articoli/2018/12/I-Tesori-di-San-Gennaro-500cbffb-9d3f-4db0-b271-8ea6e84b64f4.html — Michele Dato necklace in 1679, precious-metal craftsmanship, successive donations

**Fact-check statements:**
- The upper band of San Gennaro’s necklace was commissioned from Michele Dato in 1679  (sources: 1, 2, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- Its original upper band is described as thirteen gold links  (sources: 1, 2; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- Rulers, nobles and devotees added jewels and gifts over subsequent generations  (sources: 1, 2, 3, 4; supported_by_listed_pages_editorial_review_pending)
- The Museum of the Treasure of San Gennaro opened to the public in December 2003  (sources: 3; single_source_supported_editorial_review_pending)
- The Deputazione associated with the Royal Chapel of the Treasure traces its foundation to 1601  (sources: 3; single_source_supported_editorial_review_pending)

**Tourist/rights warning:** Ticketed museum. Use map/location story and official Via Duomo 149 address. Do not require interior photographs. The museum and Cathedral Chapel are distinct visitor experiences. Museum photographs and RAI/media footage are not automatically reusable; link to source pages without copying media or prose.

**Unresolved:**
- The museum, regional and other published pages give inconsistent years or totals for some OTHER treasures (e.g. mitre commission dates and early reliquary). Do not import those secondary numbers automatically.
- Museums may change exhibition layouts. Do not guarantee the necklace is on view at a particular time.
- The v2 Wikidata item has conflicting coordinate statements. Retain the warning and use the museum’s officially published street address for visitor directions, without inventing GPS entrance coordinates.
